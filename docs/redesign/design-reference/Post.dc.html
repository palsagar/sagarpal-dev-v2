Gray–Scott in 200 lines of WGSL

A reaction–diffusion solver written as WebGPU compute shaders, and what ping-pong buffers teach you about GPU memory.

12 October 2026 14 min read 2,140 reads Numerics, GPU

Reaction–diffusion systems are the rare corner of physics where two lines of PDE produce something that looks alive. The Gray–Scott model has two species, u and v, feeding and consuming each other on a grid.1 Change two numbers and spots that divide like cells turn into labyrinths, then into travelling waves.

1 J. E. Pearson mapped the (F, k) plane into a dozen pattern families in “Complex Patterns in a Simple System”, Science 261 (1993).

The model

Each species diffuses at its own rate. The reaction turns u into v at rate uv2, fresh u is fed in at rate F, and v is removed at rate F + k:2

2 The values used throughout follow Karl Sims's reaction–diffusion tutorial: Du = 1.0, Dv = 0.5 and Δt = 1 on a unit grid.
∂u∂t = Du ∇2u − uv2 + F(1 − u)(1)
∂v∂t = Dv ∇2v + uv2 − (F + k)v(2)

On a GPU the Laplacian is a nine-point stencil: weight −1 at the centre, 0.2 on the four edges and 0.05 on the four corners. Everything else is pointwise, which is exactly the shape of work a compute shader likes.

Ping-pong buffers

A dispatch can't safely read and write the same texture, so the state lives in two. Each step reads one and writes the other, then the bind groups swap. The whole update is ten lines:

Pack both species in one texture Keeping u and v in the red and green channels of one rg32float texture halves the loads in the stencil, and the swap stays a single bind-group flip.
